Why good therapy isn't enough: how to make sure the right clients find you

14 July 2026 · George Holloway

There are around 60,000 registered therapists in the UK. If you're newly qualified, that number can feel intimidating. If you're established, it can feel like the reason your caseload has plateaued.

But the problem most therapists face isn't competing with 60,000 people. It's being visible and memorable to the small number of people who are the right fit for them.

A client searching for therapy is usually asking one question: who understands my particular problem, and do I feel comfortable talking to them? The therapists who stand out are rarely those with the longest qualifications list. They're the ones who communicate clearly who they help and how.

Here's what that looks like in practice.

Get specific about who you help

Most therapist websites list the same presenting issues: anxiety, depression, relationships, trauma, stress, confidence, bereavement, addiction. That describes thousands of therapists. It doesn't describe you.

A therapist who says “I help men aged 40–60 who are navigating life after divorce” or “I specialise in supporting parents caring for relatives with dementia” is immediately more memorable — and easier to find. The narrower the focus, the clearer the marketing. You're not excluding people; you're giving the right people a reason to choose you.

Tell your story

Clients often choose the therapist they feel connected to before they've even made contact. A website that explains why you became a therapist, what you believe about the work, and what clients can expect from working with you will attract more enquiries than one that lists qualifications alone.

People buy trust before they buy therapy. Your story is part of what builds it.

Publish useful content

Most therapy websites are online brochures — static, unchanged, and invisible to search engines. A therapist who regularly publishes articles, guides, or videos gradually becomes recognised as someone who knows their area well.

A single well-written article — “Ten signs your anxiety is becoming avoidance” or “What carers of people with dementia tell me” — can attract the right visitors for years. It answers questions people are already asking, and it positions you as the person with the answers.

Take local search seriously

Most clients still search locally. Ranking well for “therapist in [your town]” or “counsellor in [your area]” can be more valuable than trying to compete nationally.

Many therapists neglect the basics: a complete Google Business Profile, listings on the main directories, and — where your professional body permits — collecting reviews. These aren't complicated to set up, but they make a significant difference to whether you appear when someone searches for a therapist near them.

Develop a point of view

Therapists often sound interchangeable because their websites say interchangeable things. A clear philosophy — whether that's evidence-based CBT, compassion-focused work, therapy for healthcare workers, or support for neurodivergent adults — gives people something to hold onto.

It makes you easier to remember, easier to recommend, and easier to find.

Fix your website

Most therapy websites have the same problems: generic stock photography, dense jargon, unclear fees, a difficult booking process, and no obvious next step.

A website that immediately answers five questions — who do I help, what problems do I work with, how does therapy work, what does it cost, and how do I book — already stands out from the majority. That bar is lower than it sounds, and clearing it matters more than most therapists realise.

Build referral relationships

A therapist with five strong referral relationships — a GP, a solicitor, a divorce coach, an HR department, a local charity — may see more new clients than another therapist with a larger website and no relationships behind it.

Referrals still drive a significant proportion of therapy enquiries. Building and maintaining those relationships is one of the highest-return activities an established practitioner can invest time in.

Demonstrate your expertise

Clients increasingly expect evidence before making contact. Anonymised case studies, downloadable guides, webinars, and workshops all help answer the question “why this therapist rather than another?” They show rather than tell.

You don't need all of these. One or two done well is enough to shift how you're perceived.

Respond quickly

A surprisingly simple differentiator: many therapists take several days to reply to enquiries. A therapist who responds within a few hours, offers a free fifteen-minute consultation, and makes booking straightforward will often win the enquiry over someone who doesn't.

Speed of response signals that you're organised and that clients matter to you. It's one of the easiest things to get right, and one of the most commonly overlooked.
