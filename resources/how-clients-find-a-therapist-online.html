How do clients find a therapist online — and how do you make sure they find you?

16 June 2026 · George Holloway

When someone decides they want to see a therapist, most of them do the same thing: they open a search engine and type something in. “Therapist in [their area].” “Counsellor near me.” “Anxiety therapist London.” Whatever feels most relevant to what they're dealing with.

What comes back is a list. Some directory listings, a map with local results, a handful of individual practice websites. The people at the top of that list get found. The people further down, or not on it at all, don't.

Search Engine Optimisation (SEO) is the work of making sure you're in that list. This is what it involves, and why it matters more than most newly qualifying therapists realise.

What SEO actually is

SEO is the process of making your website more likely to appear when someone searches for something relevant to what you offer. Search engines like Google rank results based on a range of factors — how relevant your content is to the search, how trustworthy your site appears, how well your pages are structured, and how your site compares to others competing for the same searches.

None of this is mysterious, but it does require some deliberate attention. A website that exists but hasn't been set up with search in mind is much harder to find than one that has.

Why it matters specifically for therapists

Most therapy practices grow through word of mouth — a GP referral, a recommendation from a friend, a colleague passing your name on. That's a reasonable way to build a caseload, but it has a ceiling. At some point, the referrals slow down, or you want to attract a different kind of client, or you move to a new area and have to start again.

Search is what fills the gap. It's the channel that works for you when you're in a session, when you're on holiday, when you're asleep. A well-optimised website brings in enquiries without you having to do anything active to generate them.

For therapists with a specialism — working with a particular client group, or using a particular modality — search is even more valuable. Someone searching for “EMDR therapist in Bristol” or “therapist for adolescents in Edinburgh” is already telling you exactly what they need. If your website makes it clear that's what you offer, and is set up to be found for those searches, those people will find you.

The basics that make the biggest difference

SEO can get complicated, but for a solo therapy practice, the fundamentals matter far more than the advanced stuff.

Your location needs to be clear. If you work in person, your website needs to say where — not just in the contact page, but in the main content. “Based in Manchester” buried in a footer is not the same as a page that's clearly written for someone looking for a therapist in Manchester.

Your specialism needs to be specific. “I work with adults experiencing a range of difficulties” tells a search engine very little. “I work with adults experiencing anxiety, burnout, and relationship difficulties” gives it something to match against real searches.

Your page titles and headings matter. Search engines pay particular attention to the titles of your pages and the headings within them. A page titled “About me” is less useful than one titled “About — Sarah Jones, Integrative Therapist in Leeds.”

Your Google Business Profile is separate from your website. When someone searches for a therapist near them, the map results that appear are pulled from Google Business Profiles, not websites. Setting one up is free and significantly increases your chances of appearing in local searches.

Directory listings contribute to how you're found. Being listed on Counselling Directory, UKCP, Psychology Today, or Welldoing doesn't just bring direct traffic from those platforms — it also signals to search engines that your practice exists and is findable, which strengthens your overall search presence.

What takes longer

The fundamentals above can be put in place relatively quickly. What takes longer is building what's sometimes called domain authority — the accumulated signal to search engines that your site is a trustworthy, established source.

This builds over time through consistent, relevant content on your site, links from other reputable sites pointing to yours, and the simple fact of having been around long enough to have a track record. There's no shortcut to this part, but getting the basics right from the start means you're building on solid ground rather than trying to fix things later.

The practical upshot

You don't need to become an SEO expert to have a therapy practice that gets found. You need a website that's clear about who you are, where you are, and who you work with — set up properly from the start, with your Google Business Profile in place and your key directories covered.

That's the foundation. Everything else builds on top of it.
