How to get more therapy clients when your caseload has stalled

18 September 2026 · George Holloway

Most therapists who want a fuller caseload start in the same place: they decide they need more marketing. More visibility, more social media, more presence. Sometimes that is right. More often, it is the wrong fix applied to the wrong problem, and six months later the number has not moved.

Say you are seeing four clients a week and you want ten. That gap is not six more enquiries. A caseload is made of four separate numbers, and the gap sits in one of them:

  1. How many people find you at all.
  2. How many of those people get in touch.
  3. How many of those enquiries become a first session.
  4. How long those clients stay.

Each of these has a different fix, and the fixes are not interchangeable. Buying more visibility when your problem is conversion means paying to send more people to something that was not working in the first place.

Here is how to tell which number is holding you back.

Do the arithmetic first

Before changing anything, find your numbers for the last three months. Most practitioners have never written them down, and the exercise takes about twenty minutes.

Count how many enquiries you received. Count how many of those became a first session. Count how many of those clients are still with you, and roughly how many sessions the average client has had.

Now work backwards from your target. If your clients stay for an average of eight sessions, a caseload of ten a week means roughly five or six new clients starting every month just to stand still once you factor in endings. If half your enquiries become clients, that means ten to twelve enquiries a month. If you are currently getting three, you have a visibility problem. If you are getting twelve and converting two, you do not.

That single calculation tells you more than any amount of marketing advice, because it tells you which of the four numbers to work on.

If almost nobody is finding you

This is a visibility problem, and it is the one most people assume they have.

The basics matter more than the ambitious things. A complete Google Business Profile, accurate listings on the directories your clients actually use, and a website that answers the question a searcher is asking will do more than a social media presence you update twice a month. Local search matters because most clients still look locally, and ranking for your town is achievable in a way that ranking nationally is not.

There is a newer route worth knowing about. A growing share of people now ask an AI assistant for a recommendation rather than scrolling a list of search results. Those assistants pull from pages that answer a specific question clearly. A page on your site that says exactly who you help and what you help with is more likely to be found and quoted than a homepage that says you offer a safe, confidential space.

If people find you but do not get in touch

This is the most common problem, and the most commonly misdiagnosed.

Someone landing on your site is deciding one thing: does this person understand my particular problem, and would I feel comfortable talking to them? They decide it fast. A site that lists anxiety, depression, trauma, relationships and stress tells them nothing, because it describes thousands of therapists.

Five questions need answering before a visitor will act. Who do you help. What problems do you work with. What actually happens in the work. What does it cost. How do they book. Missing fees is the single biggest cause of lost enquiries, because an unstated price reads as expensive and nobody wants to ask.

If your enquiry numbers are low but people are visiting, the problem is on the page, not in the search results.

If they enquire but do not book

This is usually about speed and friction rather than persuasion.

Somebody contacting a therapist has often contacted three or four. The one who replies within a few hours frequently gets the client, not because they are better but because the person is anxious and wants the decision made. Several days is normal in this profession, which makes a fast reply a genuine advantage.

The content of that first reply matters too. A short message offering two or three specific times, with a clear next step and the fee stated, converts better than a warm message that invites further discussion. You are removing decisions, not adding them.

If your conversion from enquiry to first session is below about half, this is where your caseload is leaking, and no amount of extra visibility will fix it.

If they book but do not stay

This is the number people rarely look at, and it has the largest effect.

A client who stays twelve sessions is worth three clients who stay four. If your average is short, adding new clients means running to stand still, and you will feel permanently busy while the caseload never grows.

The commercial causes, as distinct from the clinical ones, usually sit at the enquiry stage. Clients who arrive without a clear picture of what the work involves, how often you meet, or how long it might take are more likely to drift. Being explicit at the outset about how you work, and being honest when someone is not a good fit for you, produces fewer first sessions and more ongoing clients. That trade is almost always worth making.

Be careful what you buy

There are agencies that promise a specific number of new clients in a specific number of weeks. Some of them do good work. The question worth asking any of them is not how many enquiries they will generate, but which of the four numbers above they are proposing to change.

If your conversion rate is the problem, a lead generation campaign will produce more enquiries that go nowhere, at a cost per enquiry you are still paying. Volume is not the goal. Eleven well-matched clients who stay are worth more to a practice than fifty enquiries that do not convert, and considerably more to the people on the other end of them.

Where to start this week

Find your three-month numbers. Work out which of the four is furthest from where it needs to be. Fix that one thing before touching anything else.
